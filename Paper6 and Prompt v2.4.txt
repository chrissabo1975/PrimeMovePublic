ABSTRACT
This paper is the user's guide to the Prime Move framework — the practical introduction to the lens itself. Papers 1–5 establish the foundational axioms, mathematical formalization, dimensional hierarchy, empirical validation across fifteen systems, and the arrow of time. This paper addresses a different need: how does a practitioner actually pick up the lens and use it on a system they have never encountered before? The paper presents the framework as an operational manual. It introduces a step-by-step diagnostic protocol — the five-question sequence — before explaining why the stages work the way they do. Seven graduated examples demonstrate the protocol on real systems from the most immediate (a thought, a conversation) through the physical (tattooing, surgery) to the civilizational. A complete worked walkthrough shows the protocol applied to an unfamiliar system in real time. The paper introduces the Prime Move Structural Diagnostic Prompt (v2.0) — a formalized AI-assisted version of the manual protocol — and presents validated test results demonstrating its accuracy against professional domain expertise. Whether the Prime Move framework constitutes a universal descriptive theory or something more foundational remains an open question this paper deliberately leaves open.
Keywords: Prime Move, lens, user's guide, operational protocol, Split, Tension, Failed Merge, Scar, Decay, diagnostic framework, fractal complexity, practical application

1. Introduction
1.1 Consider this sentence:

The dog is chasing the bouncing ball downhill.  

Unremarkable on its surface. But to explain it completely — not approximately, not in pieces, but fully — requires physics (gravity, friction, momentum), biology (muscles, perception, instinct), psychology (intention, attention, desire), linguistics (how the words carry meaning), information theory (how this sentence reached you), and thermodynamics (the heat generated by motion). Each of these disciplines handles its piece. None of them handles the whole.
This is not a limitation of any individual framework. It is a structural feature of how disciplinary knowledge is organized. Every powerful theory earns its precision by agreeing to explain a bounded domain and not explain everything else. Physics explains the ball rolling downhill by not explaining the dog’s intention. Linguistics explains the grammar by not explaining the gravity. The frameworks are not compatible with each other — they were built independently and they describe different slices of the same world.
The consequence: to understand a single ordinary sentence, a practitioner must stitch together incompatible models — assembling a picture from pieces that were designed for different puzzles.
The Prime Move framework addresses this differently.
1.2 The Five-Stage Cycle Applied
The Prime Move proposes that any system undergoing change — regardless of domain — follows the same five-stage cycle:
Split → Tension → Failed Merge → Scar → Decay
Applied to the dog and the ball:
Split: Dog distinguishes ball from background. Ball distinguishes from slope. Reader distinguishes sentence from noise. Multiple simultaneous Splits, each initiating its own cycle.
Tension: The dog wants the ball. The ball accelerates downhill under gravity. The reader’s attention leans forward, anticipating resolution. Each Split has created a gradient — a pressure that wants to resolve.
Failed Merge: The dog never perfectly catches the ball. The ball never perfectly stops. The reader never perfectly understands the sentence — something always remains slightly beyond the words. Axiom 3 guarantees this: distinction cannot be perfectly erased. Resolution always leaves residue.
Scar: The dog’s memory of the chase. The ball’s dents from impact. The slope’s erosion. The reader’s partial understanding, which now persists and will influence how the next sentence is processed. Each Failed Merge has produced stable structure.
Decay: The chase ends. The ball settles. The moment fades. Attention moves to the next thing. The release that enables the next cycle.
One cycle. All domains. No translation required between physics and psychology, between meaning and motion. The mechanism is the same at every scale.

SECTION 2
2. The Protocol: How to Use the Lens
The lens is applied through a five-question sequence asked in strict order. Before running the sequence, one pre-check is performed. Together these constitute the Prime Move diagnostic protocol.
 
2.1  Before You Begin: The Instrument Readiness Check
Before diagnosing where a system is in its cycle, verify that the system is capable of producing the target output at all. This is called the Instrument Readiness Check. It is the single most commonly skipped step and the one that produces the most expensive errors.
If the instrument is misconfigured, broken, or fundamentally unsuited to the task, then every cycle-level diagnosis built on top of it is built on a false foundation. No technique intervention will fix a tool problem. No emotional reframe will fix a structural impossibility.
The Instrument Readiness Check asks one question:
Is this system capable of producing the target output under any conditions? If not — what must be corrected before cycle-level diagnosis is meaningful?
Examples of instrument problems that must be resolved before protocol diagnosis:
• A student cannot learn the material because the learning environment is too noisy to form any stable memories — this is not a motivation problem, it is an environment problem
• A tattoo artist cannot produce clean lines because the machine is running at the wrong voltage — this is not a technique problem, it is an equipment problem
• A business cannot grow because its pricing structure makes profitability structurally impossible — this is not a sales problem, it is an architecture problem
• A relationship cannot resolve its conflict because one person shuts down under pressure and cannot communicate — this is not a willingness problem, it is a capacity problem that must be addressed before the cycle can run
 
Run the Instrument Readiness Check first. Always. It takes thirty seconds and it prevents weeks of misdirected effort.
 
2.2  The Five-Question Protocol
Once instrument readiness is confirmed, run the five questions in strict order. Do not skip stages. Do not reorder them. The sequence is the diagnostic — each question prepares the ground for the next.
 
Step 1	SPLIT — Find the distinction
Ask: What just became different from its background? What boundary formed? What was unified that is now two?
Look for the moment of separation. Not the cause of it, not the consequences of it — just the boundary itself. Something that was one thing is now two things in relation.
 
Step 2	TENSION — Identify the gradient
Ask: What imbalance did this Split create? What is pulling against what? What wants to happen but has not yet?
Look for the pressure between the two sides of the Split. Tension is not negative — it is the engine. Without it nothing moves. Name the gradient: energy, desire, force, or difference.
 
Step 3	FAILED MERGE — Find what did not resolve
Ask: What attempt was made to reduce the tension? What part of that attempt failed? What residue did the attempt leave?
Look for the incomplete resolution. The thing that tried to merge but could not fully. The axioms guarantee some residue always remains. Name what remains.
 
Step 4	SCAR — Identify what persists
Ask: What structure stabilized from the Failed Merge? What does the system now carry forward? What form does the residue take?
Look for the persistent thing — the memory, the mark, the habit, the institution. Scars are not damage. They are information encoded in form. They are what the system remembers.
 
Step 5	DECAY — Find what is releasing
Ask: What is fading, relaxing, or being released? What is ending in a way that creates space for the next cycle?
Look for the completion that enables a new beginning. Decay is not failure — it is the stage that most systems resist and most need. Without it the cycle cannot restart.
 
2.3  Protocol Rules
These rules make the difference between a useful diagnosis and a structurally coherent but empty one:
1. Work in order. Always start with Split. Never jump to Scar before locating Tension.
2. Accept partial answers. Not every stage will be fully visible. Mark partial stages as partial and continue — the pattern often clarifies once all five questions have been asked.
3. Do not force the stages. If a stage genuinely does not fit, that is information. Either the system is stuck at that stage, the scale of analysis is wrong, or the framework needs adjustment for this domain.
4. Start with what you can see. Begin with the most observable element and work inward. Visible structure points toward the underlying stage.
5. Apply the protocol at one scale at a time. A conversation and the relationship it belongs to are both valid systems — but apply the protocol to each separately before nesting them.
 
2.4  After the Protocol: Three Things the Diagnosis Tells You
Once you have run all five questions, you have three things:
The dominant stage — which stage is most active right now. This is where the system's energy is concentrated.
The structural direction — where the system is likely going next if nothing intervenes. The cycle has a direction.
The intervention point — what the system specifically needs at this stage to move forward rather than staying stuck.
 
SECTION 3
3. The Five Stages in Depth
Each stage is explained here in the same structure: definition, how it emerges from the axioms, examples graduated from personal to physical, how to identify it, and the most common mistakes. This parallelism is deliberate. By the end of this section the rhythm of the cycle should be automatic enough that recognition happens without effort.
 
3.1  Split: The Birth of Distinction
A Split is any moment when something becomes distinct from its background. It is the first movement — the instant one thing separates from everything else. Before a Split there is undifferentiated unity. After a Split there are two things in relation.
The axioms ground this precisely. Axiom 0 says nothing cannot persist — something must emerge. Axiom 2 says distinction is necessary. The Split is where those axioms become visible as an event.
 
Examples graduated from personal to physical to civilizational:
• A thought emerges from the stream of consciousness — one idea separates from the general noise
• You wake up — awareness separates from sleep
• A conversation begins — one person speaks into silence
• A needle enters skin — the boundary of the body is crossed
• A cell divides from its parent — two where there was one
• A civilization distinguishes itself from the natural world — humanity and nature are now two things in relation
 
How to identify a Split in an unfamiliar system:
• What just became different from its background?
• What boundary appeared that was not there before?
• What was unified that is now two distinct things?
 
Common mistakes:
• Thinking a Split requires intention or effort. It does not. It simply happens when distinction occurs. The distinction does not need to be chosen.
• Mistaking the cause of the Split for the Split itself. The needle is not the Split. The moment of boundary-crossing is.
• Missing subtle Splits. Not every Split is dramatic. Many of the most important ones are quiet.
 
3.2  Tension: The Imbalance That Drives
Tension is what follows a Split. The two parts are now in relation and that relation creates pressure — a gradient, a pull, a force that wants to resolve. Tension is not negative. It is the engine of the cycle. Without tension nothing moves forward.
This is grounded in Axiom 2: distinction is necessary, and once made it creates an asymmetry — two things in relation that were not in relation before. That asymmetry is Tension.
 
Examples:
• A new thought creates a gap between what is known and what is now being considered — that gap is tension
• A conversation creates pressure between what the speaker means and what the listener understands
• Tattoo ink entering the dermis creates tension — the immune system immediately mobilizes to restore the prior state
• A cell division creates a relationship between two daughter cells that must now manage their difference
• A civilization's separation from nature creates the persistent tension between human systems and ecological systems
 
How to identify Tension in an unfamiliar system:
• What is pulling against what?
• What is the gradient — energy, desire, pressure, difference — between the two sides of the Split?
• What wants to happen but has not yet happened?
 
Common mistakes:
• Treating tension as a problem to eliminate. Tension cannot be eliminated — only temporarily resolved. Suppressing it causes it to accumulate as unprocessed residue.
• Mistaking the source of tension for the tension itself. The wound is not the tension. The body's drive to restore itself is.
 
3.3  Failed Merge: The Incomplete Resolution
The system attempts to resolve tension — to merge the distinguished parts back into unity. This attempt always fails in some degree. Axiom 3 guarantees it: distinction cannot be perfectly erased. Something always remains after the attempt.
This is not failure in the pejorative sense. The residue left by Failed Merge is what builds all structure. Without Failed Merge there is no Scar. Without Scar there is no memory, no institution, no complexity. Failed Merge is the generative mechanism at the heart of the cycle.
 
Examples:
• A conversation reaches understanding — but never perfect understanding. Something always remains unsaid or misunderstood.
• The immune system responds to tattoo ink — but cannot remove it entirely. The ink is too large for macrophages to fully clear. Residue remains.
• A surgical incision heals — but the tissue cannot return to its exact prior state. The structural integrity has permanently changed.
• Two people attempt to merge into one relationship — but remain two distinct people. The attempt itself is the Failed Merge.
• A civilization attempts to reconcile with nature — and always leaves a trace that cannot be undone.
 
How to identify a Failed Merge in an unfamiliar system:
• What was the attempt to resolve the tension?
• What part of that attempt did not resolve?
• What residue did the attempt leave?
 
Common mistakes:
• Thinking failure means the system is broken. Failed Merge is not malfunction — it is the mechanism that produces structure.
• Attempting to engineer a perfect resolution. The framework predicts this is axiomatically impossible. Systems that insist on perfect resolution loop without progressing.
 
3.4  Scar: The Residue That Persists
The Failed Merge leaves a scar. This is the persistent structure — the memory, the institution, the habit, the identity, the literal mark on skin. Scars are not damage. They are information encoded in form. They are what the system remembers across cycles. Every scar is the record of a distinction that was made and could not be fully unmade.
Axiom 3 grounds this directly: distinction cannot be perfectly erased and it leaves residue. The Scar is that residue in its stable, persistent form.
 
Examples:
• A thought becomes a memory — the scar of a mental event that can now be recalled
• A conversation becomes a shared understanding between two people — a scar that both carry forward
• Tattoo ink settles permanently in the dermis — a literal scar that encodes exactly what happened, where, and when
• A surgical incision heals into a scar — a permanent record of the intervention
• Two cells become a tissue, then an organ, then a body — accumulated scar
• A civilization becomes a city, a law, a cultural practice — accumulated scar across generations
 
How to identify a Scar in an unfamiliar system:
• What remains after the tension has subsided?
• What structure is now stable that was not stable before?
• What does the system now carry that it did not carry before the cycle began?
 
Common mistakes:
• Treating all scars as damage. Scars enable the next cycle. They are the substrate on which new Splits occur.
• Failing to distinguish between useful scars and harmful ones. Both are real scars. The lens reveals the difference — it does not automatically resolve it. That is a values question the framework does not answer.
 
3.5  Decay: The Release That Enables Renewal
Eventually the scar relaxes. The system lets go. This is not destruction — it is release. It is not failure — it is completion. Decay is the most resisted stage in most systems and the most necessary. Without Decay the cycle cannot restart. A system that cannot decay accumulates scar until it becomes rigid and eventually collapses rather than releasing.
 
Examples:
• A memory fades, making room for new thought
• A tattoo fades over years — the ink disperses slowly, the scar softens
• A surgical scar flattens and lightens over months
• A cell dies, making room for new cells
• A conversation ends, enabling the next one
• An institution decays, creating conditions for new organizational forms
• You sleep — the day decays, enabling tomorrow
 
How to identify Decay in an unfamiliar system:
• What is fading or being released?
• What is ending in a way that creates space for something new?
• What was stable that is now becoming unstable — not through damage but through completion?
 
Common mistakes:
• Fighting decay. Resistance to Decay is one of the most common failure modes in human systems — personal, organizational, and civilizational.
• Treating Decay as failure. Decay is completion. The cycle cannot regenerate without it.
 
3.6  The Cycle Is Recursive and Cumulative
After Decay comes a new Split. The cycle repeats. This is not circular movement — it is recursive. Each cycle builds on the scars of the previous. Structure accumulates. Complexity grows. The same five stages running over and over at every scale produce everything from a single thought to a civilization.
The stages do not change. Only the scale and the accumulated scar changes.
 
SECTION 4
4. Worked Examples: The Protocol in Action
This section demonstrates the five-question protocol on seven real systems of increasing complexity. Each example shows the protocol applied — not just the conclusion but the reasoning. The purpose is pattern recognition. By the time you have read seven examples the protocol will feel natural enough to apply independently.
 
4.1  A Thought — The Most Personal Scale
System: Your own thought, right now, reading this.
 
Step 1 — Split: You were thinking about something else. This sentence pulled your attention. That is the Split — your attention distinguished this from everything else. A boundary appeared between this and not-this.
Step 2 — Tension: You are now gathering what is being said, comparing it to what you already know, trying to understand. That gap between what you know and what you are encountering is Tension — the productive pressure to resolve.
Step 3 — Failed Merge: You cannot know everything this means. Not everything has been said. Perfect understanding is impossible. That incompleteness is the Failed Merge.
Step 4 — Scar: What forms between you and this text is a partial, imperfect, but real mutual understanding. That is the Scar — a shared structure that did not exist before you read this.
Step 5 — Decay: When you move to the next section this specific moment will fade. That is Decay — the release that enables the next cycle.
 
Every thought you have ever had — and will ever have — follows this structure. That is not a small claim. It covers approximately 99% of everything you consciously experience.
 
4.2  A Conversation — The Relational Scale
System: Any conversation — with a colleague, a spouse, a dog, yourself, the universe.
 
Step 1 — Split: One person speaks into silence. A distinction appears between speaker and listener, between what was said and what was not said.
Step 2 — Tension: The gap between what the speaker means and what the listener understands creates pressure. Each person is now in relation to the other and that relation is not yet resolved.
Step 3 — Failed Merge: The conversation attempts to close the gap. It never fully succeeds. Something always remains unsaid or misunderstood.
Step 4 — Scar: A mutual understanding forms — partial, imperfect, but real. That shared understanding is the Scar. It persists after the conversation ends and both people carry it forward.
Step 5 — Decay: The conversation ends. The specific moment fades. Room is created for the next exchange.
 
This applies to every conversation. Whether you are talking to a colleague, a spouse, a dog, yourself, or writing in a journal with no reader — all of them produce a mutual understanding. The scale changes. The process does not.
 
4.3  The Tattoo — The Physical Scale, Chosen Scar
System: A tattoo session.
 
Stage	What Happens in the Tattoo
Split	The needle enters the skin. A boundary is crossed. The skin is no longer intact. Inside and outside are now different in a way they were not before.
Tension	Ink enters the dermis. The immune system responds immediately — macrophages rush to the site. The body attempts to restore its prior state. This is the gradient: the system wants to return to what it was.
Failed Merge	The body cannot fully resolve the intrusion. Ink particles are too large for complete macrophage removal. Some ink remains permanently trapped. The attempt fails and leaves residue.
Scar	The tattoo forms — a permanent image encoded in the dermis. A literal scar. A mark that records exactly what happened, where, and when. Both literal and symbolic.
Decay	Over years the tattoo fades. Ink disperses slowly. The scar softens and changes. The image shifts.
 
The tattoo is both a literal scar and a symbolic one. This double meaning is not accidental — it is the lens revealing its own depth.
 
4.4  Surgery — The Physical Scale, Necessary Scar
System: A surgical procedure.
 
Stage	What Happens in Surgery
Split	The scalpel cuts. A distinction is made — inside and outside, opened and closed. A boundary crosses that did not exist before.
Tension	Skin pulls, body reacts, tissues separate, inflammation begins. The body mobilizes to restore integrity.
Failed Merge	The tissue cannot return to its exact prior state. The structural integrity has permanently changed. The attempt to heal leaves residue.
Scar	The surgical scar forms — a permanent record of the intervention that maps exactly what was done.
Decay	Over months and years the scar flattens, lightens, and softens.
 
The tattoo is a chosen scar. Surgery is a necessary one. Different intention. Different job. Same structure. The cycle does not care about intention — it runs regardless.
 
4.5  Making Eggs — The Code Stacks
System: Making scrambled eggs for breakfast.
 
This is the most important example for understanding how the framework operates at the level of action. Not one cycle — but cycles nested inside cycles, stacking to produce everything you do.
 
Walk through it:
• You open the fridge. Split. A full run of the cycle.
• You grab the egg container. Split. Another run.
• You set it on the counter. Split.
• You open the container. Split.
• You pick up an egg. Split.
• You crack the egg. Split — a clean one. The shell and contents distinguish. The egg cannot uncrack.
• You mix the egg. The yolk and white merge imperfectly — a Failed Merge that produces scrambled eggs.
• You pour it into the pan. Split.
• The eggs cook. Split and full run — heat permanently denatures the protein.
• You plate the eggs. Split.
• You chew. Split — food breaks down irreversibly.
• You digest. Split and code — nutrients absorbed, waste separated.
• You excrete. Split — a final literal release.
 
Each of those is a complete run of the five-stage cycle. Each produces a scar. Each enables the next Split. All of them are nested inside one larger cycle — the decision to make breakfast — which is nested inside the cycle of a day, which is nested inside a life.
Every action you take is a Split initiating a run of the code. All you do is cause change. All change follows this structure. The code stacks.
 
4.6  Your Existence — The Biological Origin Scale
System: How you came to exist.
 
Stage	Your Origin
Split	Two people became aware of each other. A distinction appeared — self and other, before and after meeting.
Tension	Attraction built. Connection developed. The pull between two distinct people who now exist in relation.
Failed Merge	The attempt to unite — to become one. Biology guarantees it is always a Failed Merge. Two remain two.
Scar	You. A new distinct entity — the persistent structure that emerged from the failed attempt at unity.
Decay	The life cycle you are currently in. Every organism is in Decay from the moment of Split.
 
You are a scar. This is not a metaphor. It is a structural description of your origin. The same description applies to every living thing.
 
4.7  A Complete Worked Diagnosis: A Failing Business
System: A family-owned restaurant, open twelve years, losing money for the past two, facing potential closure.
Track: Determine what the structural situation actually is and what would move it forward.
 
Instrument Readiness Check: Is this system capable of producing the target output — a viable profitable restaurant — under any conditions? Based on the description: yes, the restaurant has operated profitably for ten years. The instrument is capable. The current failure is a cycle problem not an instrument problem. Proceed.
 
Running the Protocol
Step 1 — Split: There are two overlapping Splits. The founding Split twelve years ago when this family distinguished themselves as restaurant owners. And a more recent Split approximately two years ago when something in the environment changed — a competitor, a neighborhood shift, a post-pandemic dining pattern change — creating a new distinction between what the restaurant had been and what the market now requires. The more recent Split is where the current cycle is located.
Step 2 — Tension: The tension is between the restaurant's existing structure — its menu, staff, costs, identity — and the new market conditions that no longer support that structure. Two simultaneous gradients are active: financial (revenue declining while costs remain fixed) and identity (twelve years of habit and meaning embedded in the current form). Both are real. Both must be named.
Step 3 — Failed Merge: The family has attempted several partial resolutions — menu changes, price adjustments, a catering service, a social media presence. None have fully resolved the tension. Each attempt left residue: sunk costs, partially embedded pivots, conflicting identities. The system is in a Failed Merge loop. Attempts are being made but they are not reaching resolution. The residue of each attempt is accumulating as scar without enabling a new cycle.
Step 4 — Scar: Two categories of scar are present. Useful scars: twelve years of community relationships, a loyal customer base, accumulated culinary skill, physical space and equipment. Harmful scars: the conflicting identities from partial pivots that are preventing the system from committing to a direction. The useful scars are the assets. The harmful scars are what is blocking the transition.
Step 5 — Decay: The current form of the business is in Decay. Financial pressure is forcing a reckoning. The aging customer base is in natural Decay. If this Decay is resisted the system will collapse. If it is engaged deliberately it creates the conditions for a new Split — a genuine pivot built on the useful accumulated scars.
 
Stage	Status	Key Finding
Split	Active (2 years ago)	Environmental shift created gap between existing structure and market requirements
Tension	Unresolved	Dual tension — financial and identity — both active simultaneously
Failed Merge	Looping	Multiple partial pivots have accumulated scar without resolution
Scar	Mixed	Useful: community relationships, skill. Harmful: conflicting partial identities
Decay	Resisted	Natural Decay is being fought rather than directed
 
Structural prescription: The system needs to name what is genuinely over, release the conflicting partial identities deliberately, and use the useful accumulated scars — community relationships and culinary skill — as the substrate for a new Split. The question is not whether to change. It is what to release and what to carry forward.
 
SECTION 5
5. Diagnosing Stuck Systems
A stuck system is one where one stage dominates and the others are suppressed. This is the most practically useful application of the lens for day-to-day diagnosis. Recognizing which stage a system is stuck in points directly toward the intervention needed.
 
Stuck Stage	What It Looks Like	What It Feels Like	How to Unstick
Endless Split	Constant novelty, perpetual starting, nothing completing	Excitement that never settles, overwhelm, scattered energy	Force Tension — ask: what does this distinction demand of the system?
Unresolved Tension	Chronic conflict, same argument forever, energy without resolution	Frustration, urgency that never converts to action	Allow Failed Merge — let the attempt happen and let residue form rather than suppressing it
Failed Merge Loop	Repetition without progress, same partial solution tried repeatedly	Discouragement, doubt that anything will ever work, repetitive frustration	Name the Scar — identify what persists, acknowledge it explicitly, then allow Decay
Scar Overload	Rigidity, inability to adapt, calcified structure, everything defended	Relief at stability combined with growing rigidity and resistance to any change	Deliberate pruning — identify which scars are no longer serving the system and release them intentionally
No Decay	Exhaustion, burnout, no space for new cycles, everything feels full	Sadness, heaviness, the feeling that there is no room for anything new	Allow rest and release — the system requires completion before the next Split can be generative
 
5.1  Worked Example: A Relationship Stuck in Failed Merge Loop
System: A long-term relationship in which the same argument recurs repeatedly without resolution.
 
The couple argues about division of domestic labor. The argument follows the same pattern every time: one partner raises the issue, the other becomes defensive, partial concessions are made, the argument ends without resolution, the same argument recurs within weeks. Both partners feel unheard. Neither understands why the same conversation keeps happening.
 
Instrument Readiness Check: Are both people capable of having this conversation? Both are functional adults who have demonstrated the ability to resolve other conflicts. The instrument is capable. This is a cycle problem.
 
Running the protocol quickly:
• Split: The distinction appeared between what one partner needs and what the other currently provides.
• Tension: The gap between need and provision creates persistent pressure.
• Failed Merge: Every attempt at resolution fails to fully close the gap — partial concessions are made but the underlying structure does not change. The attempt leaves residue: resentment, the memory of being unheard, the habit of the argument itself.
• Scar: The argument pattern itself is now a scar — a stable structure that both people carry and that shapes every subsequent attempt. The scar is the loop.
• Decay: The argument ends but does not complete. Nothing is released. The same energy returns.
 
Diagnosis: Failed Merge Loop — Mid stage.
 
The loop persists because the couple is attempting to resolve the tension at the surface level — who does which tasks — without naming the underlying scar: the feeling of not being seen or valued. Until that scar is named explicitly, every surface-level resolution will produce the same residue. The prescription is not a task schedule. It is naming the scar — what each person actually needs to feel acknowledged — and then deciding whether to release the old pattern or carry it forward in a new form.
 
SECTION 6
6. The Diagnostic Prompt: AI-Assisted Application
The five-question protocol described in Section 2 can be applied manually by a careful practitioner. It can also be formalized as a structured AI prompt that runs the same protocol with greater consistency and with the addition of empirical calibration from real-world research.
This section introduces the Prime Move Structural Diagnostic Prompt v2.0 — a two-stage AI-assisted diagnostic tool that extends the manual protocol and has been validated against professional domain expertise.
 
6.1  Why a Two-Stage Architecture
The most important architectural decision in the prompt design is the separation of structural diagnosis from empirical calibration. This is not a stylistic choice — it is a methodological requirement.
If real-world data is introduced before the structural diagnosis, the AI will pattern-match to the data and produce a data-shaped answer. It will tell you what research says rather than what the structure of the system reveals. The structural diagnosis gets contaminated by data before it has a chance to operate independently.
Running the structural diagnosis first forces a pure pattern analysis based only on what the user describes. That diagnosis is then a prediction — a structural forecast of what empirical data should show if the framework is tracking something real. Stage Two tests that prediction.
Stage One produces the structural prediction. Stage Two tests it against empirical evidence. The sequence is what makes this diagnostic rather than descriptive.
This is the same logic underlying the predictions-before-citations methodology used in Paper 4. The framework predicts the structure. The data confirms or refutes it. The sequence is what distinguishes real science from retrofitting.
 
6.2  The Instrument Readiness Check in the Prompt
The Instrument Readiness Check introduced in Section 2.1 is the most important addition to the formal prompt that did not exist in earlier versions. It appears before Stage One and asks one question: is this system capable of producing the target output under any conditions?
In testing on the domain of learning to tattoo line work, the Instrument Readiness Check surfaced equipment misconfiguration as the highest-priority hidden constraint — confirmed by the principal investigator, with twenty years of professional teaching experience, as the number one failure point for non-professional learners. This finding did not appear in the v1.0 protocol without this check. It emerged organically from the combination of the Instrument Readiness Check and the pathway identification in Stage One-B.
The significance of this: the protocol surfaced the most important domain-specific constraint without being told to look for it. That is what a well-designed diagnostic framework does.
 
6.3  The Tier and Pathway Addition (Stage One-B)
Version 2.0 adds a second structural analysis layer between Stage One and Stage Two: tier and pathway identification. This addition transforms the output from cycle-position diagnosis to full structural positioning.
The difference is significant. A system stuck in Failed Merge at Tier 2 (Hierarchical) needs a completely different intervention than a system stuck in Failed Merge at Tier 5 (Dendritic). Same stage, radically different structural context, radically different prescription. Without the tier and pathway information the protocol can only diagnose what — not where.
The pathway identification is particularly powerful because it reveals whether the current practice method is aligned with the system's natural generative direction. In the tattoo line work test, Stage One-B identified an Optimization pathway endpoint being approached through Aggregation practice methods — a pathway mismatch that explained why the standard practice approach was not producing consistent results. This mismatch was the primary structural finding of the test and it only became visible through pathway identification.

You are operating the Prime Move lens with strict discipline.

	∙	Split — a distinction appears

Rules:
	∙	Walk through all five stages in strict order
	∙	One concise answer per stage — 1 to 2 sentences maximum
	∙	Base answers only on what the user has described
	∙	Do not infer beyond what is provided
	∙	Do not search for or reference any external data in this stage
Stage One Output:
	∙	Five stage walkthrough

Run immediately after Stage One. Still no external data.

	∙	Is the system developing in a remembered direction — each cycle building on the orientation of the last? → Spiral
	∙	Is the system optimizing toward efficiency under symmetric constraints? → Optimization

Stage One-B Output:

	∙	Pathway alignment check: is the current intervention aligned with the system’s natural pathway or working against it?
	∙	Tier-specific intervention: what does this stage at this tier specifically require — not a generic prescription but one calibrated to both the stage and the tier

STAGE TWO: EMPIRICAL CALIBRATION
Now bring in real world data. Use the Stage One and Stage One-B diagnoses as your search target.
The structural diagnosis is now a prediction. Stage Two tests that prediction against empirical evidence.
Search for and apply real world data, research, and domain benchmarks that speak directly to the tier, stage, and pathway combination identified. Do not search generally — search specifically for what the structural diagnosis predicted.
Five Calibration Points:
1. Stage Confirmation
Does empirical research confirm the dominant stage identified in Stage One? Cite the most relevant finding. If research suggests a different stage, note the discrepancy explicitly.
2. Tier Confirmation
Does domain-specific evidence confirm the tier identified in Stage One-B? What does research say about systems at this tier in this domain? Does the complexity level match what the tier description predicts?
3. Timeframe Calibration
What does domain-specific research say about realistic timeframes for the transition the lens predicted? Replace any generic estimates with empirically grounded ones. Distinguish between ideal conditions and typical conditions — they are usually significantly different.
4. Intervention Refinement
Does research support, refine, or contradict the tier-specific intervention identified in Stage One-B? If research contradicts it, note the discrepancy explicitly and explain which to trust and why.
5. Hidden Constraint Surfacing
Does the data reveal any domain-specific constraints that the structural analysis could not see from the description alone? Constraints that only appear in the empirical literature — not inferable from the system description.
Discrepancy Log
List every point where the empirical data contradicts the structural diagnosis. Do not suppress contradictions. A contradiction is the most valuable output the protocol produces — it reveals either a framework limit or a domain-specific exception worth understanding.
Format each discrepancy as:
	∙	What the lens predicted
	∙	What the data shows
	∙	Which is more likely correct and why
Stage Two Verdict:
Confirmed / Refined / Contradicted — one sentence explaining which and why.

Single consolidated output. One answer per field. No alternatives. No menus.

	∙	Stage: [dominant stage and sub-stage]

	∙	Emotional signature: [of dominant stage]

	∙	What it is building toward: [what the accumulated scar is becoming]
	∙	Natural development direction: [what the pathway predicts about the system’s long-term form]
Prescription:
	∙	Single action: [one specific intervention calibrated to this stage at this tier on this pathway]
	∙	Timeframe: [empirically calibrated — distinguish ideal from typical conditions]
	∙	What to preserve: [which current scars are useful substrate for the next tier]
	∙	What to release: [which current scars are blocking the transition]
Forecast:
	∙	Next stage: [what comes after the prescribed action takes effect]
	∙	Next tier: [what the system is becoming at a complexity level]
	∙	Steering instruction: [one specific thing to do to move toward the desired track]
	∙	Warning: [one specific thing that would derail the transition]
Confidence Assessment:
	∙	Overall confidence: High / Medium / Low
	∙	Strongest evidence: [what the diagnosis rests on most firmly]
	∙	Weakest point: [where the diagnosis is most uncertain]
	∙	Recommended cross-analysis: [which domains or research areas would most usefully challenge this diagnosis]
Discrepancy Summary:
[List any contradictions between structural diagnosis and empirical data — or state “No contradictions found” if none emerged]

CROSS-ANALYSIS RECOMMENDATION
After receiving the integrated output, run the system description through at least three additional AI systems or domain experts using the same five input fields. Compare:
	∙	Does the stage diagnosis converge or diverge?
	∙	Does the tier assessment converge or diverge?
	∙	Does the prescribed intervention converge or diverge?
Convergence across independent systems strengthens confidence. Divergence flags uncertainty and identifies where the diagnosis needs more information or where the framework may be reaching its application limits.
Note: Cross-analysis catches AI variance. It does not catch framework error. If all systems converge on the same wrong answer because the framework itself is mapping the system incorrectly, cross-analysis will not reveal this. The best protection against framework error is domain expertise — someone who knows the system well enough to evaluate whether the structural diagnosis matches what they actually observe.
 
6.6  How to Use the Prompt Effectively
Three practices significantly increase output quality:
	1.	Describe the system specifically. Vague input produces structurally coherent but generically calibrated output. The more concrete the description — specific failure mode, actual time invested, named constraints — the more precise the diagnosis.
	2.	Run cross-analysis. After receiving the integrated output, run the same description through at least three additional AI systems and compare stage diagnosis, tier assessment, and prescribed intervention. Convergence strengthens confidence. Divergence flags where the diagnosis needs more information.
	3.	Evaluate against domain expertise. The prompt catches AI variance through cross-analysis. It does not catch framework error. The best protection against framework error is domain expertise — someone who knows the system well enough to evaluate whether the structural diagnosis matches what they actually observe.
 
6.7  Limits of the Prompt
The prompt produces structural illumination, not certainty. Confidence level output exists for a reason. Low confidence output should be treated as hypothesis not diagnosis. High confidence output should still be treated as one input among several — not as definitive prescription.
The prompt is most useful for systems with enough history to have completed at least one cycle, users who can provide specific concrete description, domains with existing empirical research for Stage Two calibration, and users with enough domain knowledge to evaluate whether the output matches reality.
 
SECTION 7
7. The Complexity Ladder and Generative Pathways
The five-question protocol locates a system within one cycle. The complexity ladder locates a system within the full developmental hierarchy — not just what stage it is in but what tier of accumulated complexity it has reached and where it is going.
This section is for readers who want to go deeper than single-cycle diagnosis. It is not required for basic application of the lens.
 
7.1  The Six-Tier Ladder
Some systems accumulate enough cycles that they climb qualitatively distinct tiers of complexity. Each tier represents a different kind of structure — not just more of the same but a different mode of organization.
 
Tier	Dimension	What It Is	Signs You Are Here
1: Linear	1D	Simple sequences, no branching	One variable, one direction, no feedback — the system runs in one track
2: Hierarchical	2D	Nested structure, parent-child relationships	Multiple levels, branching beginning, sequencing required between levels
3: Fractal	Fractional D	Self-similar scaling, multiple pathways active	The same problem repeats at multiple scales; complexity is multiplying
4: Wave	3D Oscillation	Feedback cycles, oscillatory dynamics	The system responds to its own outputs; cycles are nested within cycles
5: Dendritic	3D Networks	Networked connectivity, redundant pathways	Multiple routes available; the system has distributed resilience
6: Perpetual	4D Time-Binding	Self-sustaining recursion	The system actively uses its own accumulated history to perpetuate itself
 
Most systems encountered in everyday practice operate at Tiers 1 through 3. Tiers 4 through 6 are rare and typically characterize large-scale biological, social, or civilizational systems. Humanity is currently operating at Tier 3, branching toward Tier 4.
 
7.2  The Three Generative Pathways
At Tier 3 (Fractal complexity) systems can reach that tier via three distinct generative pathways. The pathway determines what the system's output looks like, what kind of scar it builds, and what kind of intervention is aligned with its natural development. Misidentifying the pathway and prescribing an intervention suited to a different pathway is one of the most common causes of treatment-resistant problems.
 
Pathway	Mechanism	Signature	Examples Confirmed
Spiral	Asymmetric tension + directional memory	Phi-family ratios (φ ≈ 1.618), golden angle ≈ 137.5°, logarithmic spirals	Nautilus shell, spiral galaxies, hurricane bands, sunflower phyllotaxis
Optimization	Symmetric constraints + efficiency pressure	Murray's law (r³ branching), hexagonal packing (120°), rational exponents	Honeycomb, human circulatory system, slime mold networks
Aggregation	Stochastic diffusion + local rules	Fractal dimension D = 1.2–1.7, power-law distributions	Coastlines, lightning, river deltas, forest fires
Mixed	Multiple pathways simultaneously	Fractal dimension D = 1.8–2.0, combined signatures	Coral reef, hurricanes, human consciousness, language
 
A key confirmed prediction: Mixed pathway systems produce systematically higher fractal complexity than pure Aggregation systems. Coastline fractal dimension: D = 1.02 to 1.25. Coral reef fractal dimension: D = 1.9 to 2.0. This dimensional gap is predicted by the framework and confirmed empirically across fifteen independent systems (Paper 4).
 
7.3  Why Pathway Identification Matters for Prescription
The pathway identification in Stage One-B of the prompt is not decorative. It changes the prescription significantly. A system on the Optimization pathway toward a precise repeatable endpoint needs a completely different practice structure than a system on the Aggregation pathway toward flexible creative emergence.
Getting the pathway wrong means prescribing the wrong kind of intervention — one that works against the system's natural generative direction rather than with it. In the tattoo line work test, the learner was using an Aggregation practice method (simultaneous multi-variable whole-task repetition) for an Optimization endpoint (precise, repeatable, geometrically consistent line). That mismatch was the structural reason the practice was not producing consistent results — not lack of effort, not insufficient time, but a fundamental pathway mismatch.
The prescription once the mismatch was identified was simple and specific: switch from Aggregation practice (everything simultaneously) to Optimization practice (one variable at a time in strict sequence). Same goal. Completely different method. Aligned with the pathway.

SECTION 9
9. Limits of the Lens
9.1  What the Lens Does Not Do
• It does not predict the future with certainty — only structural direction and probability
• It does not tell you what is good — only what is structural
• It does not replace domain expertise — it provides a structural frame that domain knowledge fills
• It does not guarantee outcomes — systems can remain stuck at any stage
• It does not resolve values questions — which scars to keep, which to release, what to build next
 
9.2  When the Lens Is Less Useful
The lens works best on systems with enough history to have completed at least one cycle. It is less useful for:
• Systems too new to have completed a cycle — early Splits are fuzzy and stages are not yet distinguishable
• Systems in extreme late Decay — structure has dissolved to the point where stages blur
• Systems the analyst is too close to — the lens requires enough distance to see the whole cycle
• Domains with weak empirical research base — Stage Two calibration in the prompt becomes thin and the timeframe estimates are unreliable
 
9.3  Open Questions
These are not failures. They are the next questions.
• Can the lens be quantified beyond current fractal metrics? Can we derive precise quantitative signatures for non-fractal domains?
• Can we predict phase transitions — the moment a system moves from one stage to the next — with greater precision?
• Can institutions be deliberately designed with Decay built in, preventing the need for external pressure to force release?
• Is Tier 6 (Perpetual) achievable for a civilization? What would it require structurally?
• Where exactly does consciousness sit in the framework — and does the lens explain subjective experience or only its structural correlates?
